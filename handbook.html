Chapter 01

Culture

SICA exists because most people never get to practise the conversations that decide things. School grades what you know. It almost never grades whether anyone understood you. That gap is the entire reason this club is here, and every rule below exists to protect it.

The one sentence that decides everything

The standing rule

When a decision is not covered by this handbook, choose the option that makes the most nervous person in the room more likely to speak next week.

That is not a slogan. It is a tiebreaker, and it resolves most arguments an officer will actually have. Should we run a competition? Only if losing would not silence someone. Should we post photos? Only if the people in them chose to be there. Should we call on someone who did not volunteer? No.

Three rules for every room

  1. Nobody performs before they are ready. Everything starts in pairs. Moving from a pair to a small group to the whole room is a choice the member makes, never one an officer makes for them.
  2. Feedback is specific and forward-looking. The only permitted format is what worked and one thing to try. No rankings, no "you seemed nervous", no comparisons between members.
  3. What is said in the room stays in the room. Members practise on real situations. Repeating someone's example outside the meeting is the fastest way to end this club, and is grounds for removal.

What SICA is not

  • Not a competition. No scores, no winners, no rankings of members against each other. The moment practice becomes performance, the shy members stop coming and only the confident ones remain — which is precisely the failure mode SICA was built to avoid.
  • Not therapy. Officers are students, not counsellors. Chapter 6 covers exactly what to do when a conversation goes somewhere an officer is not qualified for.
  • Not a résumé line. Officers who joined for the title will be visible within a month, and Chapter 4 covers what happens then.

Tone

SICA is calm and specific. It does not hype, exaggerate, or invent statistics. In writing and in the room, the standard is: plain enough for a 9th grader with no context, concrete enough that they can picture it. Restraint is the trust signal — a club that oversells reads as less credible, not more.

Chapter 02

Expectations

Vague expectations are how student clubs die quietly. Every number below is deliberately small enough to keep during a hard week of school.

What every officer owes

CommitmentStandardWhy this number
Weekly meetingAttend all. Two unexcused absences per semester is the limit.Five officers means one absence is 20% of leadership missing.
Prep timeAbout 45 minutes a week outside the meeting.Small enough to survive AP season. If a role needs more, the role is wrong.
Reply timeWithin 24 hours on school days.Not "immediately". Officers have classes.
Materials readyBy 6pm the night before.So the President can fix a gap without a panic that morning.
Notice to step backTwo weeks, in person.Leaving is allowed. Disappearing is not.

What members owe

Almost nothing, on purpose. The barrier to attending must stay at zero, because the members SICA most wants are the ones most likely to talk themselves out of coming.

  • Show up when you can. There is no attendance requirement and no penalty for missing weeks.
  • Participate at your own pace. You may pass on any activity without explaining why.
  • Keep what other people share inside the room.
  • Give feedback in the format from Chapter 1. This is the only hard rule for members.
Do not add a membership requirement

The instinct to require attendance for "real members" will appear around week eight, when numbers dip. Resist it. A dip is normal; a requirement converts a low-pressure club into another obligation and accelerates exactly the decline it was meant to fix.

What officers owe each other

  • Say no early rather than yes and then disappear. A declined task is a scheduling problem; an abandoned one is a broken meeting.
  • Raise a problem with the person before raising it with the group.
  • Cover for an officer having a bad week, and expect it back.
Chapter 03

Hiring

SICA selects officers rather than electing them in year one, because a founding team chosen by popularity will not survive the work. From year two onward, Chapter 8 governs.

The process

  1. Open the role publicly. Announce at a meeting and in the group chat. Never fill a role by private invitation only — that is how a club becomes a friend group with a constitution.
  2. Short written application. Three questions, no résumé, no GPA. The questions are below.
  3. One fifteen-minute conversation. Two officers present, never one. One asks, one takes notes.
  4. Trial task. Give the candidate one real piece of work with a one-week deadline — draft a run of show, write a week's discussion questions, make one flyer.
  5. Decide within three days of the trial and tell everyone who applied, including the people you did not pick.

The three application questions

Ask exactly these Describe a conversation you handled badly. What would you do differently?
Question two Someone in the room has not spoken in three weeks. What do you actually do?
Question three What is a week where you would have to tell us you cannot do your SICA work?

The third question is the most useful one on the page. A candidate who claims no such week exists is either not being honest or does not understand their own schedule, and both predict the same failure.

What you are selecting for

  • Follow-through over charisma. The trial task tells you more than the interview. A quiet officer who delivers beats a compelling one who does not.
  • Warmth toward beginners. Ask yourself whether the most nervous member in the room would approach this person. If not, they cannot hold a SICA role regardless of talent.
  • Willingness to be corrected. Watch how they take a note during the trial task.
Automatic disqualifiers

Repeating something a member shared in a meeting. Mocking a member's attempt, including as a joke. Wanting the title primarily for college applications and saying so when asked directly. Any of these ends the application regardless of how good the rest is.

Reference

Role expectations

What each officer actually owns, what it costs in hours, and how anyone can tell whether it is being done. Applicants see the same text on the application form, so nobody takes a role without having read what it is.

Chapter 04

Removal

Most student handbooks skip this chapter, which is why most student clubs carry a non-functioning officer for a full year. Removal is written down here so that doing it is a procedure rather than a personal betrayal.

The two categories

Not delivering is a performance problem and gets the full ladder below. Breaking a room rule — repeating what a member shared, mocking someone, making the room unsafe — skips the ladder and goes straight to step four.

Step 1
Private conversation

The President, in person, not over text. Name the specific miss, ask what is going on, agree one concrete fix. Most cases end here, and usually the cause is school, not commitment.

Step 2
Written expectation

One short message confirming what was agreed and by when. This exists so that step 3 is not a surprise and cannot be argued about later.

Step 3
Reduce the role

Before removing anyone, try shrinking the job. An officer drowning in AP season can often still own one thing well. A smaller role kept is worth more than a large one abandoned.

Step 4
Removal

Decided by the President plus one other officer, with the advisor informed. Told in person, privately, before it is announced to anyone else.

How to actually say it

The removal conversation This isn't working, and I think you know that too. I'm taking you off the officer team. You're still welcome at every meeting as a member, and I mean that. Here's what I'm telling everyone else: that you stepped back. Nothing more.

Three things make that script work. It is direct, so the person is not left guessing. It keeps the door open, because losing a member as well as an officer helps nobody. And it settles the public story in the same conversation, which prevents the version where they hear their own news secondhand.

The announcement rule

The club is told that someone stepped back from their role. Reasons are never announced. If a member asks directly, the answer is "that's between them and me" — every single time, including when the reason was bad.

After a removal

  • Recover the assets first — files, logins, drafts. Do it the same week, without ceremony.
  • Redistribute the work explicitly. Silent absorption is how the next officer burns out.
  • Reopen the role using Chapter 3. Do not leave it vacant and quietly do it yourself.
Chapter 05

Meetings

One meeting a week, 75 minutes, same room and same time all year. Predictability is a feature: a member deciding whether to come should never have to check anything.

The standard run of show

3:30Welcome and attendanceSecretary
3:40The idea — one practical concept, taught brieflyPresident
4:00Paired practice — everyone, at the same timeVice President
4:30Reflection — what worked, one thing to tryVice President
4:45Closing and the weekly challengePresident

The 20-minute lesson cap is deliberate. The instant a lesson runs to 30 minutes, the meeting becomes a class, and SICA's entire claim is that it is not one. If the idea cannot be taught in twenty minutes, it is two weeks, not one.

Everyone practises at once

Paired practice happens simultaneously across the whole room, never as pairs taking turns in front of everyone. This single choice is what keeps the club safe for nervous members: with fifteen conversations happening at once, nobody is being watched.

Officer duties during a meeting

  • President — teaches the idea, holds the clock, closes.
  • Vice President — runs the activity, pairs people, watches for anyone left out.
  • Secretary — attendance, notes, writes the minutes into the Library within 48 hours.
  • PR Officer — one announcement slot, the reminder post before the next meeting, and photos during meetings — only with consent, and never during a practice activity.
The pairing rule

The Vice President assigns pairs. Members do not choose their own partners after week two — self-selected pairs recreate the social groups the member already has, which is the one thing SICA can offer that they cannot get elsewhere.

Officer-only meeting

Fifteen minutes, weekly, immediately after the members leave. Three questions and nothing else: what worked today, what broke, who owns the fix. Anything longer belongs in the group chat, and anything contentious belongs in Chapter 6.

Cancelling

A meeting is cancelled only for a school closure or losing the room. Never for low expected attendance — running a meeting for four people is how you still have a club in November. Cancel by 8am, in every channel.

Chapter 06

Conflict resolution

A club about communication that handles its own conflict badly has no credibility. This chapter is the club practising what it teaches.

The escalation ladder

Level 1
Direct, within 48 hours

The two people talk, in person. Most conflicts are a misread message and dissolve here. Waiting longer than 48 hours is what turns a misunderstanding into a position.

Level 2
Mediated by the President

Both people present. The President does not rule on who is right; they get each side stated in the other person's words first, then ask what each needs going forward.

Level 3
Officer team decides

For anything affecting how the club runs. Majority of officers, President breaks a tie. The decision is recorded in the minutes; the argument is not.

Level 4
Faculty advisor

Anything involving safety, harassment, or a member's wellbeing goes here immediately and skips every level above. This is not a failure of the officer team — it is the correct action.

The line officers do not cross

If a member discloses self-harm, abuse, or anything involving their safety, officers do not handle it, advise on it, or keep it confidential. It goes to the faculty advisor or a counsellor the same day. Officers are students. This is the one place where being helpful means handing it to an adult.

Conflict between a member and an officer

The member takes it to any officer who is not the one involved, and that officer brings it to the President. A member should never have to raise a complaint with the person it is about. If the President is the officer involved, it goes to the Vice President, and if necessary to the advisor.

When two officers disagree about a decision

Disagreement is not conflict, and this club should have more of it than most. The rule is argue, decide, commit: say everything before the decision, and once it is made, nobody relitigates it in front of members. An officer who keeps reopening a settled decision in public is a Chapter 4 problem, not a Chapter 6 one.

Opening a hard conversation I want to talk about what happened on Thursday, and I want to start by checking I understood it right. Can you tell me how it looked from your side?

The written-down rule

If a decision only exists in a group chat, it did not happen. Decisions go in the meeting minutes, in the Library, the same week. This is the single habit that most determines whether next year's officers inherit a club or a rumour.

Who speaks for SICA

  • To the school — the President only, with the advisor informed.
  • Publicly, in posts and flyers — the PR Officer, using the voice rules in Branding. The President has final say on anything published.
  • To parents — the President, and only alongside the advisor.
  • To another club — any officer may talk; only the President may agree to anything.

Writing standard

Everything SICA publishes follows the same rules as the website: short sentences, concrete examples, no jargon, no exclamation marks, no invented numbers, no urgency tactics. If a sentence would not make sense to a 9th grader who has never heard of the club, rewrite it. The full do-and-don't list lives in Branding.

Announcing something that went wrong

Say what happened, what is being done, and by when. No apology paragraph, no explanation of whose fault it was. A cancelled meeting gets one sentence, not three.

Chapter 08

Elections

Year one is appointed under Chapter 3. From the end of year one onward, officers are elected under this chapter. The shift from appointment to election is itself the proof that SICA is an institution rather than one student's project.

JunHandover complete before summerChapter 9

Who may vote and who may stand

  • Vote: any member who has attended at least four meetings that year. Four is low on purpose — it is enough to know the club, and low enough not to exclude people who joined late.
  • Stand: any member who has attended at least eight, and is not graduating that year.
  • President: must have served a full year as an officer. This is the one role with a hard prerequisite, because the President carries the curriculum.

How the vote runs

  1. Anonymous, on paper or a form. Never by show of hands — a club about speaking up should understand exactly why that matters.
  2. One vote per role. Highest count wins; a tie is decided by the outgoing officer team, not by a re-vote.
  3. The advisor counts, or watches the count. No candidate counts their own race.
  4. Results announced the same day, to candidates first and privately, then to the club.
The uncontested-role problem

Most roles will have exactly one candidate, and some will have none. An uncontested candidate still requires a confidence vote — a majority of "yes". A role with no candidate is left vacant and its duties are split in writing; it is never quietly absorbed by the President, which is how one person ends up being the whole club again.

Chapter 09

Succession

This is the most important chapter in the handbook. High school clubs do not usually die of bad ideas; they die when the person holding everything graduates. SICA was founded by one student who currently writes the curriculum, runs the meetings, owns the brand and holds every login. That is the single largest risk to the club's existence.

The test

If the President stopped coming tomorrow, could the club still meet next Thursday? Ask it at the end of every month. When the answer is no, whatever caused that is the most important thing to fix.

De-risking the founder, starting now

  1. Somebody else teaches by week four. The Vice President writes and teaches a full session in the first month — imperfectly is fine. A club where only the founder has ever taught is a founder's project.
  2. Two people hold every login. Email, site, workspace, social accounts. No exceptions, from day one.
  3. Written beats remembered. Any process an officer keeps in their head gets written into the Library the week it is noticed.
  4. Recruit one year below. At least one officer should be a year or more from graduating. Otherwise the club has an expiry date built into its leadership.

The handover pack

An outgoing officer is not finished until this exists in the Library. It is the deliverable of leaving.

  • Every login, transferred and confirmed working by the incoming officer.
  • Every file they own, moved into the Library and named so a stranger can find it.
  • A one-page role note: what the job actually is, what surprised them, what they would do differently.
  • The list of people they dealt with — advisor, admin contacts, anyone outside the club.
  • One conversation with their successor, in person, unrushed.

Shadowing month

Through May, the incoming officers run the meetings and the outgoing officers sit in the room and say nothing. Watching your replacement do it differently and staying quiet is the last duty of the job — and the first real evidence that the club is bigger than whoever started it.

What the founder specifically must let go of

Written plainly, because it will be the hardest part: the curriculum, the final say on branding, and the habit of fixing things personally rather than assigning them. A founder who still approves every post in year two has not built an institution — they have built a job that outlives their interest in it.

Chapter 10

Scaling

SICA breaks in a specific way as it grows: the paired-practice model that makes it safe stops working when the room is too large for one Vice President to pair and watch. Each threshold below is the point where something must change.

SizeWhat changesWhat breaks if you don't
Under 12Nothing. Run it exactly as written.Nothing. This is the ideal size for the model.
12–25Add a second person running activities alongside the VP.Pairs go unwatched; a member sits out and nobody notices.
25–40Split reflection into two circles in the same room, one officer each.Reflection becomes a lecture and only the confident speak.
40–60Two sections on different days, same lesson, same week.The room stops being safe. This is the real ceiling of one room.
60+Train members as facilitators. Officers run officers.Officers burn out and quality collapses within a term.
Growth is not the goal

Forty members who attend once are worth less than fifteen who come back every week. If a growth decision would make the room less safe for a nervous member, it fails the standing rule in Chapter 1 and the answer is no — including when the number would look better on an application.

When to add a role

Add a role when a specific job has been dropped twice, not when the org chart looks thin. Roles created before the work exists produce officers with titles and nothing to do, and those officers stop coming — which then reads as a failure of the club rather than of the structure.

What never scales

  • Everyone practises at the same time. If growth would force pairs to perform in front of the room, the club has grown wrong.
  • The feedback format. More members means more untrained feedback, so the rule gets stricter as you grow, never looser.
  • The 20-minute lesson cap. More people is never a reason to teach longer.

The one metric worth tracking

Not attendance — return rate. Of the people who came last week, how many came back? A club growing at 60% return is failing louder than a club holding steady at 85%. It is the only number that tells you whether the room is actually working, and it should be reported at the officer meeting every week.

Amendments

Changing this handbook

Any officer may propose a change. It passes with a majority of officers and the advisor informed. Chapter 1's standing rule and Chapter 6's safety line cannot be amended by officers at all — they require the advisor's agreement, because they are the two things protecting members rather than the club.

Record every amendment in the minutes with the date and the reason. A handbook that changes without a trail is a handbook nobody trusts.
